\section{General Notes}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=4pt]
    \item Nations are inherently ``imagined'' or otherwise products of conception rather than explicit lines: most members of a nation will never meet most of the other members, but still a sense of commonality exists. The nation must be limited, sovereign, and considered as a community; these align with four portions of the definition of the nation:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Imagined (not the same as fake -- just that it cannot be readily conceived in its entirety as a face-to-face community would)
        \item Limited / Exclusive
        \item sovereign
        \item Exists as a community
    \end{enumerate}

\end{itemize}


\section{Important Specific Factual Claims}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=4pt]
    \item Nationalism exists on a separate dimension from fascism, liberalism, etc. -- emerged from the decline of a religious / dynastic justification for peoplehood that declined in the Enlightenment period and continued therafter
    \item A great deal of nation-building was facilitated by the development of effective communication technology (i.e. printing press) and capitalistic markets, which facilitated exchange particularly by means of standardized (and particularized) language
    \item Colonial empires developed strategic nationalistic characteristics primarily to protect dynastic and aristocracies by creating a common vision of peoplehood
    \item Origins largely from Latin American Creole cultures
    \item Three primary institutions which supported this construction particularly within colonial societies:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Censuses: allowed for distinct, mutually exclusive counts and categorizations
        \item Maps: allowed for distinct borders and the creation of commonly understood delineations
        \item Museums: display of stories of peoplehood and the origins of the nation
    \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}
