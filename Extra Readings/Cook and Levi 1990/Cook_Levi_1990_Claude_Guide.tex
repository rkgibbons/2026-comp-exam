\section{Central Claims}
%--------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=4pt]
    \item The volume is an interrogation, from broadly sympathetic insiders,
    of \textbf{how far rational choice explanation can be pushed} in the social
    sciences, and of where and why it breaks down. The contributors are not
    external critics dismissing rational choice; the project is to locate its
    boundary conditions.
    \item The organizing move is that the standard rational actor
    model---self-interested, consistent preferences, maximizing over known
    options---is a \textbf{useful idealization whose failures are systematic
    rather than random}. Because they are systematic, they are themselves
    theorizable, and the interesting work lies in specifying when the model
    holds and what supplements it when it does not.
    \item Recurring candidate limits across the volume: \textbf{cognitive
    constraints} (bounded rationality, framing effects, heuristics);
    \textbf{norms, values, and commitments} that are not reducible to
    instrumental calculation; \textbf{social structure and embeddedness}, which
    shape both preferences and available options; and \textbf{collective action
    and cooperation} outcomes that narrow self-interest predicts poorly.
    \item A methodological through-line: the tension between
    \textbf{methodological individualism} and explanations that require
    supra-individual entities (norms, institutions, structures) to do real
    explanatory work.
\end{itemize}

\section{Why It Sits on the Reading List}
%--------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=4pt]
    \item It is a \textbf{hinge text} for questions about the microfoundations
    of comparative politics. Much of your list runs on rational choice
    machinery; this volume asks what that machinery can and cannot carry.
    \item Levi's own research program---on consent, compliance, and
    quasi-voluntary cooperation with the state---makes her a useful bridge
    between rational choice and the institutional and normative accounts
    elsewhere on the list.
    \item Cook brings the social exchange and social structure tradition in
    sociology, which supplies the ``embeddedness'' critique in a form more
    rigorous than the usual gestures at culture.
\end{itemize}

\section{Connections to Your Other Readings}
%--------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=4pt]
    \item \textbf{Olson 1965}: the canonical statement of the collective action
    problem that this volume complicates. Where Olson derives free-riding from
    narrow self-interest, the limits-of-rationality literature asks why
    cooperation is nonetheless so common.
    \item \textbf{Ostrom 1990} (same year): the strongest empirical answer to
    that question---common-pool resource users building institutions that
    sustain cooperation. Reading these two together is the natural pairing:
    Ostrom shows the anomaly, this volume theorizes it.
    \item \textbf{North 1990} (also same year): institutions as constraints on
    choice, plus North's own concession that ideology and informal norms do
    explanatory work that pure rational choice cannot.
    \item \textbf{Scott 1985}: peasant resistance as behavior that looks
    irrational under a narrow model but is intelligible once norms, dignity,
    and risk are taken seriously.
    \item \textbf{Putnam 1993}: social capital and trust as the kind of
    supra-individual resource that strains methodological individualism.
    \item \textbf{Svolik 2012} and \textbf{Kalyvas 2006}: both push rational
    choice into hard cases (authoritarian power-sharing, wartime violence);
    useful for arguing about where the model earns its keep.
    \item \textbf{Weber}: the \textit{Zweckrational} / \textit{Wertrational}
    distinction is the ancestor of this entire debate---instrumental versus
    value rationality, with the latter irreducible to the former.
\end{itemize}

\section{Likely Exam Uses}
%--------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=4pt]
    \item As the \textbf{citation for the boundary condition}: when an answer
    leans on a rational choice mechanism, this volume is the acknowledgment
    that the mechanism has known limits, which is usually worth more than
    pretending it does not.
    \item As \textbf{a bridge between literatures}, letting you move from
    rational choice arguments to institutional or normative ones without the
    transition looking like a change of subject.
    \item In answers about \textbf{microfoundations}: what does a macro-level
    claim about regimes or institutions actually assume about individual
    behavior, and is that assumption doing legitimate work?
\end{itemize}
